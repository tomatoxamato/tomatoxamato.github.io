# The Resource Is Present

### Six machines for manufacturing scarcity

Every object follows one rule:

> The resource is already available. The mechanism does not produce it—it merely withholds it until the user pays, works or submits.

That distinction makes the capitalism critique structural rather than decorative. The machines would make the point without the surface gestures that decorate them—the spikes, timers and gauges. Those details reinforce the critique; they do not carry it.

## 1. The Wage Bottle

A transparent bottle filled with water, enclosed in a soft, pink, thorn-covered sleeve. Its drinking valve opens only after the user turns a resistant hand crank a set number of times.

Every 30 rotations releases one small serving. The mechanism is transparent: the crank does not pump or filter the water, it only spins a useless flywheel before withdrawing a simple latch.

Two gauges show:

- how much water is present;
- how much water the user is currently permitted to access.

**Statement:** Labor does not create the water. Labor satisfies the institution controlling it.

**The three grammars:**

- **Brutalism:** exposed gears, latch, reservoir and construction.
- **Hostile architecture:** a drinking vessel that obstructs drinking.
- **Nuclear semiotics:** repeated pictograms show water → barrier → labor → ration.

## 2. The Daily Bread Safe

A transparent breadbox framed in raw cast aluminum, resembling a combination of a supermarket display, prison food hatch and bank vault. An entire loaf is plainly visible behind an anti-theft grate.

To receive one slice, the user inserts and stamps a miniature timecard. The mechanism advances the loaf exactly one slice toward a narrow opening. The user can always see that more food exists than they are allowed to receive.

The timecard records hours "worked" rather than money spent, so the exchange between labor and survival is explicit.

**Statement:** Hunger can exist beside abundance when access (not supply) is scarce.

**The three grammars:**

- **Brutalism:** visible hinges, rails, fasteners and cutting mechanism.
- **Hostile architecture:** the box provides food while preventing access to it.
- **Nuclear semiotics:** carved images communicate abundance, enclosure, rationing and hunger without text.

## 3. Pay-Per-View Window

A window blind made from heavy raw-aluminum louvers. Daylight is visible between them, but the blind normally remains closed.

To open it, the user must punch a timecard and turn a deliberately inefficient flywheel. Once opened, a timer begins counting down. After several minutes, the louvers close again even when abundant daylight remains outside.

Its exposed counterweight reveals that the blind could easily remain open. The labor requirement exists solely because the product was designed to demand it.

**Statement:** The sun is free; access to it has been placed behind a manufactured condition.

**The three grammars:**

- **Brutalism:** the counterweights and unnecessary resistance mechanism are exposed.
- **Hostile architecture:** an architectural opening is redesigned to deny its own purpose.
- **Nuclear semiotics:** sun, eye, barrier, clock and labor symbols are cast into the louvers.

## 4. The Leasehold Planter

A transparent planter containing edible herbs, vegetables or fruit. Its roots, soil and water are all visible, but the plant is enclosed by a cage of blunt ceramic thorns.

A token opens one small harvesting hatch for a limited period. The cage then closes, regardless of how much food remains.

Around the soil are buried ceramic markers showing a sequence:

> seed → rain → sun → plant → fence → token → food

The markers could be removed and distributed like archaeological artifacts. Someone encountering one without the original product could still reconstruct the story of a naturally growing thing being enclosed and rationed.

**Statement:** Nature performs the production; ownership captures the result.

**The three grammars:**

- **Brutalism:** soil, irrigation and structural components remain visible.
- **Hostile architecture:** the product invites cultivation while preventing harvest.
- **Nuclear semiotics:** durable distributed markers preserve the narrative of enclosure.

## 5. The Eight-Hour Chair

A domestic sofa modeled on a hostile public bench. Dividers break it into narrow, sloping seats that prevent reclining or lying down.

A foot treadle is mounted in front of it. After the user performs a symbolic "shift" (eight minutes rather than eight hours), the dividers retract and the seat levels out. It remains comfortable for one minute before returning to its hostile configuration.

A transparent panel reveals that the mechanism's default position could be comfortable. Springs have been deliberately added to restore discomfort.

**Statement:** Rest is physically available but culturally treated as something that must be earned through productivity.

**The three grammars:**

- **Brutalism:** the rails, springs and counterweights are exposed.
- **Hostile architecture:** it directly repurposes anti-sleeping bench design.
- **Nuclear semiotics:** standing body → labor → seated body → reclining body → expulsion.

The chair extends the argument beyond material resources: time, comfort and rest become necessities placed behind a labor gate.

## 6. The Heat Lease

A low-temperature radiant panel is already producing warmth, but it sits behind an expanded-metal cage with heavy steel louvers.

The user turns a separate crank to open the louvers for one minute. The crank does not power the heater; it only operates the barrier. When the allotted time expires, the louvers close and redirect the warmth away from the user.

Two exposed meters distinguish between:

- heat being produced;
- heat being permitted through.

A thermal-sensitive surface behind the closed louvers changes color, so the trapped abundance stays visible.

**Statement:** The resource has not disappeared when access is terminated. It remains physically present on the other side of a contractual barrier.

**The three grammars:**

- **Brutalism:** raw thermal mass, exposed pipes, meters and louvers.
- **Hostile architecture:** a heater engineered to prevent its warmth from reaching people.
- **Nuclear semiotics:** body, cold, heat, enclosure and timed-access pictograms.

## Shared visual language

To read as one collection rather than six unrelated objects, the machines share a single design system.

### Transparent abundance

Water, food, light, plants and heat should remain visible. The viewer must never be able to blame the problem on genuine scarcity.

### Two meters

Every object has two adjacent gauges:

- **PRESENT**
- **PERMITTED**

The first remains high while the second falls to zero. These could eventually be represented through pictograms rather than English.

### Visible unnecessary labor

The crank, pedal or timecard should visibly operate a lock, not produce the resource. A useless flywheel, friction brake or timer makes the manufactured inefficiency explicit.

### Pink silicone thorns

The bottle's pale-pink spikes could become a recurring motif. They communicate hostility, but in a soft, purchasable consumer color.

That creates an additional critique:

> The system not only builds barriers around necessities, it sells the appearance of those barriers back to us as lifestyle design.

### A wordless five-part narrative

Each product carries a durable relief showing:

1. the resource;
2. the human body needing it;
3. the artificial barrier;
4. the demanded labor or payment;
5. the measured ration.

The same sequence could be stamped into metal, cast into concrete, buried as ceramic tokens and printed in the instructions. That is the nuclear-semiotic "defense in depth."
